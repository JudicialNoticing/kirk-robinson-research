# Discussion Card — Evaluating the Kirk/Robinson Case as Laymen

*A one-sitting distillation of the full framework. Everything here can be said out loud mid-conversation.*

---

## Before any evidence: three things to agree on

**1. Neither of us has the full picture, and the gaps aren't random.**
The prosecution, the defense, and the investigators all hold material we don't. Filings get sealed, discovery isn't public, the defense stays quiet on purpose, agencies release on their own schedule, and what reaches us online is filtered for attention, not accuracy. So a missing piece of evidence only means something if you can say why it *should* be public by now. "You can't prove it" is true of nearly everything, in both directions, and settles nothing.

**2. Each of us says our theory, and what would change our mind, before we start.**
Out loud. What do you think happened, roughly how confident are you, and what would move you? If neither of us can name anything that would move us, we're not doing what we think we're doing.

**3. The question is which story explains the evidence better — not whether a story is possible.**
Almost anything is possible. The useful question is: given everything we can see, which account predicts it better, and by how much?

---

## Know what you're actually holding

Three levels. Say which one you have when you cite something.

- **Level 1** — A document or sworn statement you've read yourself: filings, affidavits, testimony, transcripts.
- **Level 2** — A named official or named reporter saying it on the record.
- **Level 3** — Everything else: anonymous sources, social media, clips, screenshots, "reports say."

**The one rule that matters most:** an article *about* a document is Level 2, not Level 1. "The affidavit says" means you read the affidavit. If you read a story about it, say so. This is the most common mistake on every side of this case.

**Two more:**
- Level 3 can raise a question. It cannot settle one.
- Level 1 doesn't mean *true*. A prosecutor's filing is the prosecution's argument. It's accountable and attributable, not neutral.

---

## How to weigh evidence

**Does it actually tell our two theories apart?**
Most facts are exactly what both theories would predict. Those facts are true, and they're worth zero. Identify them and skip them — they're where most of the time gets wasted.

**Where does it come from?**
Ten headlines from one press conference is one piece of evidence, not ten. Before counting your sources, ask whether they all trace back to the same place — the same document, the same witness, the same statement. Expect your list to shrink. That's the check working.

**How often does this happen normally?**
Early reports being wrong, timelines shifting, officials correcting themselves, details changing — this happens in nearly every major case with heavy coverage. Something is only suspicious if it's stranger than the normal amount of strange. Before calling anything an anomaly, ask what it looks like in cases nobody disputes.

**Is it a real contradiction or noise?**
Different wording, rounding, "about" — not contradictions. Early reports later corrected — usually not. Two sources genuinely conflicting — worth resolving. A claim that conflicts with physical reality (distance, timing, geometry, what a camera could see) — that's a real one, because physics doesn't have a motive.

**How surprised would you be if you were wrong?**
For each item, both of us say how expected it is under our *own* theory, not just under the other person's. This is the question people most avoid asking about their own side.

---

## Two traps to name out loud when they happen

**Screwups aren't plots.**
Incompetence, bureaucratic self-protection, bad communication, and deliberate concealment all look the same from outside: inconsistencies, revisions, defensiveness, delay. Evidence that the response was sloppy is not evidence for any *particular* alternative story. You need something the alternative story predicts and the sloppiness story doesn't.

**More detail makes a story less likely, not more.**
A story with ten specific moving parts feels more convincing than one with three, and is mathematically less probable. Every added "and then" is another thing that has to be true. This applies to the official version too.

---

## Keep four questions separate

1. **Who did what, when, how**
2. **Was the official response competent and honest**
3. **Why**
4. **What it means politically**

These are different arguments with different evidence. When they blur — and they will — either of us can call it.

---

## Ground rules

1. Say what level you're holding when you cite something.
2. Same standard for sources you agree with.
3. State the other person's theory in its strongest form before attacking it.
4. Keep a running tab of every extra assumption each of us adds to keep our theory alive. The side adding more, faster, is being rescued, not confirmed.
5. "I don't know" is a legitimate answer to a sub-question. Neither of us owes a complete account.
6. If you've moved, say so.
7. Drop your single best piece of evidence. Does your view survive? If not, you're resting on one thing — say so.

---

## Optional: make it testable

If a ruling, filing, or unsealing is expected, each of us writes down what we expect it to show and how confident we are. Check afterward. It's the only way to find out which of us is actually calibrated, and it costs nothing.

---

**The goal isn't agreement.** It's leaving the conversation able to say exactly where we disagree — which item, which assumption, which prior — even if we never resolve it. A disagreement pinned down that precisely is a good result.
